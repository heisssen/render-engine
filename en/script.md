## COLD OPEN

NARRATOR: [quiet] Berlin. The first of August, nineteen fourteen. Eleven at night. The Kaiser has already gone to bed.

NARRATOR: He gets up, throws on a coat, and receives the chief of his General Staff. He hands him a telegram from his cousin, the King of England. Then he says one sentence.

KAISER: [strong German accent] [agitated, curt] Now you can do what you want.

NARRATOR: Helmuth von Moltke goes back to work, and seven of Germany's eight armies roll west, into Belgium, toward France. Four years and roughly ten million dead soldiers later, three empires are gone, and the twentieth century has its script.

NARRATOR: [slower] Moltke believed something about that night for the rest of his life. If one telegram had reached the palace half an hour earlier, he wrote, the Kaiser would never have signed the order at all. So let's give history that half hour.

NARRATOR: Tonight, Germany turns its army around and goes east, against Russia. No Belgium. No march on Paris. That single switch would have erased some of the most famous names, books and borders of the last hundred years.

## STING

## ACT I · THE TRACKS

NARRATOR: To see why this isn't fantasy, you need one piece of trivia that most people never hear. In nineteen fourteen Germany had one war plan — because a year earlier, one man threw the other one away.

NARRATOR: The famous one, the one everybody learns in school, went west. While Russia, huge and slow, was still gathering its army, Germany would swing through neutral Belgium, walk around the French fortresses, take Paris in about six weeks, and then put the whole army on trains and deal with the Russians.

NARRATOR: The other plan did the opposite. It came from the elder Moltke. His uncle — same name, same job, different century — was the field marshal who beat France in eighteen seventy. His logic was simple. France has fortresses, Germany has fortresses, so sit behind them in the west. Then hit Russia, together with Austria, while the Russians are still unpacking.

NARRATOR: And this plan wasn't a doodle on a napkin. The General Staff kept it up to date, year after year, with the trains, the timetables, the lot. It was called the Great Eastern Deployment. In nineteen thirteen, the younger Moltke threw it out.

NARRATOR: [dry] From that point on, Germany could not fight Russia without first invading France. Nobody outside the General Staff had a vote on this. Not the Chancellor, not the Foreign Office, not the Navy, not even Germany's ally in Vienna. As the historian Holger Herwig has shown, there was no fallback.

NARRATOR: Now, the men who held these plans were family. The Kaiser, King George of Britain, and Tsar Nicholas of Russia were first cousins — grandsons and in-laws of Queen Victoria. In the last week of July, the Kaiser and the Tsar wrote to each other in English, in telegrams signed "Willy" and "Nicky". Nicky asked to send the quarrel to the court at The Hague. Willy never answered that one. On the first of August, Willy sent his last.

KAISER: [strong German accent] [matter-of-fact] Immediate, affirmative, clear and unmistakable answer from your government is the only way to avoid endless misery.

NARRATOR: That evening, at five o'clock, the Kaiser signed the order to mobilize. And then, as Moltke was driving back to his office, a car came after him. Return to the palace. Important news.

NARRATOR: The news came from London. The German ambassador, Prince Lichnowsky, had wired that the British Foreign Secretary seemed to be offering a deal. If Germany left France alone, Britain would stay out, and might even keep France out too. The Kaiser and his Chancellor were, in Moltke's words, joyfully excited. The Kaiser turned to him.

KAISER: [strong German accent] [pleased] So we simply deploy the whole army in the East!

NARRATOR: Moltke said no. The deployment of a million men, he said, cannot be improvised. It was a full year's work, and once it starts, it cannot be changed.

MOLTKE: [strong German accent] [measured] If His Majesty insists on leading the whole army east, he will not have an army ready for battle. He will have a wild heap of disorderly armed men without supplies.

NARRATOR: [beat] The Kaiser was very annoyed. And he said the sentence that Moltke later wrote down himself, because it hurt him so much.

KAISER: [strong German accent] [cold] Your uncle would have given me a different answer.

NARRATOR: They compromised, sort of. The deployment would run west as planned. But the Kaiser, without asking Moltke, sent a telegram to a division near Trier, ordering it not to cross into Luxembourg. Moltke went home and, in his own words, shed tears of despair. When the order was brought to him to sign, he refused.

NARRATOR: [amused] Meanwhile, in Luxembourg, a detachment of the 69th Infantry Regiment had already taken the railway station at a village called Troisvierges. They wrecked the station's telegraph. Then the stop order reached them, and they marched back out. The war had started, paused, and would start again the next morning, on one railway platform.

NARRATOR: Because at about eleven, the reply from London arrived. King George wrote that he knew of no such offer.

GEORGE: [calm, British, courteous] I think there must be some misunderstanding as to a suggestion that passed in friendly conversation between Prince Lichnowsky and Sir Edward Grey.

NARRATOR: And that's how we got to the coat thrown on at eleven at night, and "now you can do what you want".

NARRATOR: [serious] Here is the strangest part. Moltke may have been wrong about the trains. In nineteen twenty-five, General Hermann von Staabs, who had run the General Staff's railway department before the war, read Moltke's memoirs and was so offended on behalf of his old office that he wrote a whole book to answer them.

NARRATOR: His claim: given the order on the evening of August first, four of Germany's seven armies could have been standing against Russia by the fifteenth of August, with three left to hold the west. Two weeks. At the border, Russian tracks were three and a half inches wider, so troops would have to re-lay gauge mile by mile. But Staabs had already factored that into the timetables. Historians have argued ever since, but nobody has proven he was wrong.

NARRATOR: [lighter] So here is our switch. The telegram from London reaches the palace half an hour earlier. The Kaiser digs in. Moltke says he cannot answer for it, and the Kaiser lets him go. And the railway men do what they did best. They turn the trains around.

## ACT II · THE OTHER TRACK

NARRATOR: Let's start in the west, because France doesn't get a vote either. France has an alliance with Russia, so France fights. And the French army does what it had planned to do for years. It attacks.

NARRATOR: Picture the geography of the frontier. Without Belgium, the front shrinks to less than half its real length. Instead of seven hundred kilometers from the Channel to Switzerland, you get a corridor of under three hundred, between Luxembourg and Switzerland. And that corridor is a wall: Fortress Metz, fortress Strasbourg, and the Vosges mountains.

NARRATOR: In the real August, French infantry went into this kind of ground in bright blue coats and red trousers, charging straight into entrenched machine guns. On the twentieth of August, nineteen fourteen, at Morhange in Lorraine, the French lost about five thousand men killed in a day. Two days later, mostly in the Ardennes and at Charleroi, they lost around twenty-seven thousand dead in a single day. In our timeline, the Germans are waiting behind reinforced concrete, with fewer kilometers to hold and nothing else to do. It's the same slaughter, only worse.

NARRATOR: There is one thing France gets to keep. In real history, the Germans grabbed northern France in the first weeks, and with it most of France's iron ore and almost two-thirds of its pig iron. Here, that industry stays French, and France fights better armed for years. So the west can't lose this war. It just can't win it either.

NARRATOR: Which brings us to the biggest question in the whole scenario. What does Britain do?

NARRATOR: In real life, the British cabinet argued for a week. Most ministers opposed intervention until German boots crossed into Belgium, violating the neutrality treaty of eighteen thirty-nine. Belgium gave the doubters their answer.

NARRATOR: [thoughtful] In our timeline, nobody touches Belgium. There is no "poor little Belgium" on the front pages, no stories of burning towns, no queues of volunteers outside recruiting offices. So we'll pick the middle road. Britain stays out in nineteen fourteen. It sells, it lends, it watches. And it comes in only when it's frightened enough.

NARRATOR: Staying out also forces Britain to confront itself. Without a continental war to freeze Irish Home Rule, London is pushed into immediate crisis in Ulster. But the decisive drama of nineteen fourteen unfolds in the east.

NARRATOR: [energetic] Now the east. This is where it gets interesting. Russia had promised France to attack Germany fast, and it keeps that promise. Two Russian armies march into East Prussia.

NARRATOR: In reality, one German army was waiting for them there, and even that one army destroyed a Russian army at Tannenberg. The Germans took about ninety-two thousand prisoners. The Russian commander, General Samsonov, walked into the forest and shot himself.

NARRATOR: In our timeline, there are four German armies in East Prussia. Both Russian invasions are smashed, one after the other, and the road into Russian Poland is open. In the autumn the Germans push from the north and the Austrians from the south, out of Galicia. A pincer, with far more men than the real one ever had. Warsaw falls before Christmas.

NARRATOR: And here is a consequence almost nobody thinks about. In the real nineteen fourteen, Austria-Hungary lost more than four hundred thousand men in three weeks in Galicia and surrendered Lemberg. In our version, massive German reserves arrive weeks earlier and limit the catastrophe, so Austria finishes its first year battered, but not broken. And an intact Austria is a very different ally.

NARRATOR: The Ottoman Empire still joins Germany, as it really did. Its secret treaty with Berlin was aimed at Russia, so its war is with Russia, in the Caucasus. Italy, looking at a strong Austria next door, decides to stay neutral.

NARRATOR: [dry] Which means the Middle East you know is never made. With Britain not at war with the Ottomans, there is no Gallipoli. No landing at Anzac Cove, and no Lawrence of Arabia financing an Arab revolt.

NARRATOR: It rewrites two famous careers as well. Mustafa Kemal never wins the mythic fame on the Gallipoli ridges that carried him to founding modern Turkey; here, he remains an officer fighting in the snow of the Caucasus. And in London, Winston Churchill keeps his desk at the Admiralty, because there are no Dardanelles beaches to gamble on.

NARRATOR: [slower] Nor are there secret wartime treaties dividing the Levant. The borders of Iraq, Syria, Jordan, and Lebanon are never drawn this way. For London, an intact Ottoman Empire allied with a dominant Berlin is a permanent dagger pointed at the Suez Canal and the route to India.

NARRATOR: [serious] One tragedy cannot be erased. In nineteen fifteen, the Ottoman regime carried out the murder of over a million Armenians. Historians trace those decisions directly to the war with Russia, which still rages here. Alternate history cannot tell us those lives would have been spared.

NARRATOR: Nineteen fifteen. In the west, the French keep attacking the same wall near Metz. Gas, shell shortages, hundreds of thousands killed for a few kilometers. Their commander, Joffre, asks his government for permission to go around, through Belgium. [dry] He had asked the same thing in nineteen twelve, and been refused, because it would cost France the British. He's refused again.

NARRATOR: In the east, the great summer offensive of nineteen fifteen arrives with crushing force. The Russian fortresses fall one after another. The last is Novogeorgievsk, where up to ninety thousand men surrender. Russia loses Poland, Lithuania, and Courland. By autumn the front runs from Riga through Minsk to Rovno.

NARRATOR: In October, Bulgaria joins Germany and Serbia is overrun. And in January, nineteen sixteen, the first through train runs from Berlin to Constantinople, past stations hung with flags. It's called the Balkan Express, and for a German imperialist, it's the spine of a new Europe.

NARRATOR: Now, America. In the real world, you know how this goes. In May nineteen fifteen, a German submarine sinks the liner Lusitania off Ireland. Nearly twelve hundred people die, a hundred and twenty-eight of them American. Two years later, the Zimmermann telegram offers Mexico Texas, New Mexico and Arizona, and America goes to war.

NARRATOR: In our timeline, Britain isn't blockading Germany in nineteen fifteen, so Germany has no reason to starve Britain with submarines. The Lusitania is a neutral nation's liner, and it docks in Liverpool on schedule. For most Americans, this is somebody else's European family feud.

NARRATOR: Nineteen sixteen. Remember Verdun, the battle that killed about three hundred thousand men in ten months and became the symbol of this war? [beat] In our timeline, it doesn't exist. The Germans aren't attacking anyone in the west. They're sitting in concrete.

NARRATOR: Instead, it's France that's breaking. And in the spring of nineteen sixteen, Britain is finally forced in. German submarines begin escorting Ottoman supply convoys through the eastern Mediterranean, and the Admiralty warns that a combined fleet could soon contest the Suez Canal. That is the line London will not let anyone cross. The British arrive late, short of shells, with a divided cabinet and no cheering crowds at the stations. With them comes the empire, including the Indian Army, which in real history had already held the line at Ypres in nineteen fourteen.

NARRATOR: Together, the British and the French stage their own Somme, in Lorraine. Weeks of bombardment, ten or fifteen kilometers gained, and hundreds of thousands of casualties. The tanks the British are secretly prototyping arrive at the front a year later than they really did — too few, too late to break German concrete in Lorraine. And some French regiments start refusing to attack a year earlier than they really did.

NARRATOR: On the eastern front, there's no Brusilov offensive. The Austrians hold. Romania stays out. The Germans take Riga, reach the approaches to Kyiv, and stop. There's no need to go further. Russia is coming apart on its own.

NARRATOR: Because this war wasn't only lost in trenches. It was also lost in kitchens and in banks.

NARRATOR: In real history, the British blockade starved Germany down to a thousand calories a day in the infamous Turnip Winter. In our timeline, the blockade starts a year and a half late, and there is grain flowing in from occupied Poland. Germany is hungry, but it doesn't break.

NARRATOR: Britain, meanwhile, is burning through money. In our timeline, London has been financing France since nineteen fourteen, and now has to pay for its own late army. In real history, Britain survived by buying American munitions and wheat on credit through J.P. Morgan until Washington entered the war. But here, with America firmly neutral and the Federal Reserve warning banks against foreign Treasury bills, British credit runs dry. No rescue is coming from across the Atlantic.

NARRATOR: [serious] And in Petrograd, there are bread queues. Prices are several times higher than before the war. On International Women's Day, nineteen seventeen, the women from the bread lines march. Within days the garrison refuses to fire on the crowds, and the Tsar abdicates in his train at Pskov.

NARRATOR: In real history, the new government kept fighting, and lost everything to Lenin. In our timeline, with German troops near Kyiv and Riga, and no French front pulling them away, the new government does the thing the old one never dared. It asks for an armistice.

## ACT III · THE TRACK TAKEN

NARRATOR: Nineteen seventeen. Russia signs a separate peace. It gives up Poland, Lithuania, Courland, and fifty German divisions get on trains heading west. France now faces the entire German army, with Britain a year into a war it never wanted, and America at home.

NARRATOR: And here Germany makes its smartest move. It offers peace with no annexations in the west. We took nothing from you, so let's just go home. By the end of the year, delegates are meeting in neutral The Hague.

NARRATOR: By late nineteen seventeen, the war reaches its natural conclusion: a compromise peace. Borders in the west return to nineteen fourteen. Germany asks for nothing from France or Britain, and in return, keeps its new empire in the east. On paper, an honorable draw. In practice, a colossal German triumph.

NARRATOR: And here is the strangest part of this victory: we don't have to invent the map. Germany actually built it in real life, in nineteen seventeen and nineteen eighteen.

NARRATOR: Poland became a kingdom under German and Austrian protection, with a regency council, and no king. Lithuania elected a Catholic German prince, the Duke of Urach, as King Mindaugas the Second. The Baltic German nobles offered their crown to the Kaiser himself. Finland elected the Kaiser's brother-in-law as its king. In Kyiv, the Germans installed a Hetman.

NARRATOR: [dry] In real history, none of those kings ever reigned. Most of them never even visited. When Germany lost in November nineteen eighteen, the whole ring of crowns from Helsinki to Kyiv evaporated within weeks. In our timeline, Germany doesn't lose. So King Mindaugas the Second gets a coronation, and every one of those crowns has a German army standing behind it.

NARRATOR: [serious] This was not a gentle arrangement. The occupation zone the Germans called Ober Ost was run like a military estate: forced labor, requisitions, travel passes between districts. And when Germany signed its Bread Peace with Ukraine in nineteen eighteen, Vienna and Berlin expected about a million tonnes of food. About a hundred and thirty thousand tonnes crossed the border, and Germany's share was forty-nine thousand.

NARRATOR: Now the people. Alternate history is really about them. Where do the men who shaped the twentieth century end up?

NARRATOR: Lenin. In real history, Germany shipped him to Russia in a sealed train in nineteen seventeen, specifically to ignite a revolution and knock Russia out of the war. In our timeline, Russia is already out. Nobody needs him. He sits in Zurich, writing obscure articles about why the revolution hasn't come yet. Stalin stays a forgotten exile in Siberia, and Trotsky an émigré journalist in New York.

NARRATOR: [quiet] Adolf Hitler volunteers for the Bavarian army in August nineteen fourteen. In our timeline, his regiment holds a static trench in Lorraine. The war ends with a German victory. There is no Versailles treaty, no myth of an army stabbed in the back. Most likely, he returns to Munich with a minor medal, a small veteran's pension, and no national humiliation grand enough to build a movement on.

NARRATOR: But the war didn't just break empires and spare dictators. It wrote twentieth-century literature. J.R.R. Tolkien fought on the Somme as a signals officer. Years later he wrote, in a foreword, that by nineteen eighteen all but one of his close friends were dead. He had started inventing Middle-earth before the war, so that world would still exist. But if his war happens in Lorraine instead, The Lord of the Rings is a different book, from a different battlefield.

NARRATOR: Ernest Hemingway drove an ambulance for the Red Cross in Italy and was wounded at the Piave, two weeks before his nineteenth birthday. In our timeline, Italy is neutral. There's no Piave, no Caporetto, and no A Farewell to Arms. And All Quiet on the Western Front, if somebody writes it at all, is written by a Frenchman.

NARRATOR: [quiet] And one poem. In May nineteen fifteen, after the second battle of Ypres, a Canadian army doctor, John McCrae, wrote In Flanders Fields, about the poppies growing between the crosses. That poem is the reason millions of people in Britain, Canada, Australia and New Zealand pin a red poppy to their coats every November. In our timeline, there's no war in Flanders, and Canada isn't fighting in nineteen fifteen. The poppy is just a flower.

NARRATOR: Now the twist. Who loses this war? France. More than a million dead, and nothing to show for it. The army will say it was betrayed by the politicians, and by Britain, which came late. The story Germany told itself after nineteen eighteen, France now tells itself.

NARRATOR: France has done this before. After eighteen seventy-one, the statue of Strasbourg on the Place de la Concorde stood draped in black for decades. France already has a royalist, nationalist movement, Action Française, with the doctrine. It already has veterans' leagues with the men. [slow] On the sixth of February, nineteen thirty-four, in the real world, those leagues rioted on that same square and brought down a government in a single night. In our timeline, they've had fifteen extra years of grievance.

NARRATOR: Even the heroes change. In real history, Philippe Pétain became the victor of Verdun, and in nineteen forty, that reputation made him the head of the Vichy regime. In our timeline there's no Verdun, so no victor of Verdun. And a young captain named Charles de Gaulle, who was bayoneted and captured at Verdun in nineteen sixteen, never is.

NARRATOR: The same goes for the heroes of the next war. A German lieutenant called Erwin Rommel won the Pour le Mérite, Prussia's highest decoration, in the mountains above Caporetto in nineteen seventeen, and later turned his war into a bestselling book, Infantry Attacks. Hitler read it. In our timeline, Italy is neutral, so there is no Caporetto, no Pour le Mérite, and no book.

NARRATOR: [thoughtful] Does Germany get a peaceful twentieth century? Probably not. A German bloc from the Rhine to Kyiv, a humiliated France, a Russia that wants its land back, and a Britain that never quite made up its mind. Germany's own soldiers come home and demand reforms, and the empire slowly turns into a parliamentary monarchy. The nineteen forties may well bring war back to Lorraine and the Dnieper, with tanks and aircraft this time. But it would be a different war, between different enemies, and it would not be the war of annihilation we know.

NARRATOR: [honest] Could this whole scenario have broken down? Of course. Russia could have retreated instead of attacking. France could have sat tight behind its fortresses. Staabs may have been too optimistic about his trains.

NARRATOR: [quiet] What stays with me is how thin the thread was. Moltke was probably right that you couldn't turn a million-man army around in one evening. He was a careful man, doing the sensible thing.

NARRATOR: [slow] And sometimes the most expensive mistakes in history are the ones that look the most sensible at the time.

NARRATOR: So what do you think? Would Germany have won this war, or does Russia swallow it like it swallowed everyone else? [beat] Moltke said the trains couldn't turn around. Soon on Switchyard, we'll look at the timetable he was protecting, and how it dragged a continent into war.
